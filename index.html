Research.

Symbiosis

Discovery of a novel relationship where a shelled amoeba houses a flagellate

Shelled amoeba have been studied since the 1800s in different parts of the world but despite their extensive study, there was no record of them housing a flagellate. This study was the first to report a shelled microbial eukaryote housing a flagellated microbial eukaryote. Overall, this study identified an unknown microbial partnership in shelled amoeba, identified a previously undescribed flagellate species, and it shows how modern methods like single-cell sequencing can reveal microbial relationships that traditional methods like microscopy and culturing are ill suited for.

Published at Journal of Eukaryotic Microbiology

Computational tool development

Rethinking large-scale phylogenomics with EukPhylo v1.0

Phylogenomic analyses for microeukaryotes are challenging for two reasons. The first is that single-cell sequencing generate a mixture of sequences from hosts, associated microbiomes, and contaminants, and the second is that most studies rely on resampled gene sets from previous studies which could lead to bias in results. EukPhylo address these challenges by providing a database of over 15,000 gene families for replicable gene family assignment and a phylogeny-informed contamination removal method for removing contaminants.

Published at MBio

Limits of life

Discovery of the "fire amoeba" which that holds the world record for the highest heat tolerance of any complex life form

High temperatures lead to the breakdown of membranes, proteins, and other biomolecules needed by living cells to survive thereby killing them. For decades, it has been thought that no eukaryotic cell could survive above 62 degrees Celsius (144 degrees Fahrenheit). The discovery of this organism proves this to be false, and changes our understanding of the limits of life. Studying extremophiles like this could provide potential ways of engineering durable enzymes capable of surviving high heat, and guide our search for extraterrestrial life.

Published at Cell

Movement of Eukaryotes

Evolution of movement at the microbial scale

Myosins are known to be involved in movement and motility in organisms across the tree of life. This could range from a well defined muscle contraction in humans to a temporary pseudopod projection in some microbes. Their evolutionary origin in some clades is however not fully resolved. This project aims to determine the origin of some myosin proteins used by microbes for movement.

Status: In progress

Molecular Clock

Timeline of eukaryotic evolution

The origin of eukaryotes represents a large leap on the complexity of life, ultimately generating organisms that range from a microscopic single-celled amoeba to a large multicellular whale. Yet we still face data and analytical barriers to understand when these innovations first appeared. Using our phylogenomic pipeline EukPhylo, I aim to provide a more refined timeline of when eukaryotes – cells with nuclei, including animals, plants, fungi, and countless microbes - first appeared on earth and diversified into major clades.

Status: In prep

Genome Evolution

Challenging the theories of codon usage evolution in eukaryotes

I am analyzing patterns of codon usage evolution across the eukaryotic tree of life to see what extent the current “rules” apply to microbial eukaryotes.

Status: In progress
